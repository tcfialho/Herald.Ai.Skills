# Discover a worthwhile improvement

Use **only when the request leaves the outcome or opportunity materially open**, or a new finding warrants explicitly revisiting scope. A comparison with existing high-quality references can define the intended outcome without a numeric target; do not turn a clear "make X comparable to Y" request into a question about which cosmetic detail to change. The aim is to choose a valuable refinement of the existing artifact, not to generate a product roadmap. A category such as "optimize" need not identify which outcome to prioritize. For a resolved target, skip this protocol. For a request for advice only, present ranked opportunities and evidence without editing.

## 1. Observe the actual use

Identify who or what consumes the artifact, its main job, and a representative path or output. Read the request, existing requirements, tests, documentation and available feedback. Run, render, read or exercise that path. Identify what already works and what must remain. If access or observation is missing, try a safe representation or existing evidence; label remaining uncertainty. Do not invent a baseline, user preference or demand. If an explicit goal or strong usage evidence resolves the choice, proceed and state any scoped assumption. If competing outcomes or taste depend on a consequential unexpressed preference, ask the smallest discriminating question or present reversible options; do not block on details that can be observed or safely inferred. Ask for access only when necessary.

Look across relevant lenses, not a mandatory checklist: correctness/reliability, friction/clarity, performance/resource cost, consistency/quality relative to a reference, maintainability when it affects real work. A product may benefit from actual usage evidence, support tickets or comparison with a relevant alternative; use external research only if it resolves a consequential unknown. Do not infer market demand from competitor features. A document, algorithm or design needs no market analysis.

## 2. Choose the opportunity, not yet the solution

Keep a small set (typically 2–4 if genuinely distinct). Combine duplicates by underlying problem. For each, capture:

- **Observed gap:** concrete location, case, output or measurement; distinguish confirmed defect from hypothesis.
- **Why it matters:** affected user/workflow or intended outcome; frequency/severity when known, otherwise describe scope and uncertainty.
- **Possible mechanism or next diagnostic:** not a commitment to a feature; note protected strengths and potential trade-offs.
- **Evidence of success:** what better would look like, feasible comparison and remaining unknown; estimated time and risk.

Choose the highest-value feasible opportunity: significance to the actual goal, strength of evidence, plausible headroom, reversibility, risk and verification cost. Do not rank cosmetic changes above consequential defects just because they are easy to measure. Conversely, if the request is aesthetic, prioritize aesthetic quality, not unrelated maintenance. If choices are materially preference-dependent, ask or offer alternatives; otherwise make and record a reasonable scope assumption. Avoid invented numeric precision or scoring demand.

## 3. Hand off immediately to refinement

Use a small fraction of the available budget for discovery (roughly 10–20% as a starting heuristic); stop earlier when a grounded, testable direction is clear. If uncertainty dominates, make the next action a short diagnostic rather than speculative implementation. If the request is too broad to cover, choose one meaningful slice and state what is outside it. Explicitly distinguish observation, hypothesis and proposed acceptance criteria. If no reliable opportunity can be found, say what evidence is missing; do not manufacture one to fill the budget.

Record the selected opportunity, why it wins over alternatives, and non-goals. Convert it into the normal contract (outcome, preservation, evaluation, budget), then return to the main loop. The discovery may provision a representative baseline but must not hide failed cases or alter acceptance after seeing candidates. Preserve unselected ideas if useful; do not execute them without scope. Resume an existing contract rather than rediscovering the goal each iteration. New functionality is in scope only when the user's request and agreed intent support it; otherwise recommend it separately.
